\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Depth versus breadth.} A single hidden layer of sufficient width can give an approximation of any continuous
function~\cite{cybenko1989approximation,hornik1989multilayer}, yet depth-separation results show functions
that deep networks represent far more efficiently than shallow ones~\cite{eldan2016power,telgarsky2016benefits},
and width has its own limits~\cite{lu2017expressive}. These results concern representational
capacity. We ask the practical question that follows from them: at a fixed, small parameter budget, which
arrangement trains to the lowest error, and which one runs fastest?

\textbf{Multi-branch and shared-trunk designs.} Parallel branches are pivotal to Inception~\cite{szegedy2015going}
and ResNeXt~\cite{xie2017resnext}, and wide residual networks trade depth for width~\cite{zagoruyko2016wide}.
A shared trunk with several heads is the hard-parameter-sharing pattern of multi-task
learning~\cite{caruana1997multitask,ruder2017overview}, and averaging several predictions underlies deep
ensembles~\cite{lakshminarayanan2017simple}. Our Parallel, \HA, and \HC models are small examples of these
ideas.

\textbf{Modular networks.} Mixtures of experts route inputs to specific sub-networks~\cite{jacobs1991adaptive}
and scale to very large models~\cite{shazeer2017outrageously}. Neural module networks combine
modules to mirror the compositional structure of a query~\cite{andreas2016neural}, and a recent survey
notes the advantages and costs of modularity~\cite{pfeiffer2023modular}. Whether neural networks utilize
compositional structure at all is an open question~\cite{hupkes2020compositionality}. \HB tests the simplest
version of this hypothesis, showing one module per stage of a known composition joined by dense
connections~\cite{he2016resnet,huang2017densenet}.

\textbf{Measuring efficiency.} Parameter count and FLOPs are known to be poor proxies for latency: ShuffleNet~V2
derived design guidelines from directly measured speed~\cite{ma2018shufflenetv2}, and Dehghani et
al.\ showed that the choice of cost indicator can reverse model rankings~\cite{dehghani2022efficiency}.
Hardware-aware architecture search therefore optimizes measured latency~\cite{tan2019mnasnet,cai2019proxylessnas,elsken2019nas}.
Standard benchmarks such as MLPerf Inference~\cite{reddi2020mlperf}, MLPerf Tiny~\cite{banbury2021mlperftiny},
and DAWNBench~\cite{coleman2019dawnbench} fix the measurement rules, and surveys of efficient processing
explain where time goes in DNN inference~\cite{sze2017efficient,canziani2016analysis}. Efficient
architectures for restricted devices~\cite{howard2017mobilenets} motivate studying very small models.
Benchmarks are also fragile. Framework choice alone changes measured speed~\cite{shi2016benchmarking}, seed and
data-split variance can exceed the effects being claimed~\cite{bouthillier2021accounting,henderson2018deep},
and empirical ML has a history of overstated conclusions~\cite{lipton2019troubling}. Our pilot study
(\cref{sec:pilot}) shows a concrete small-scale example of the first two problems.

\textbf{Positioning.} Prior latency studies target large vision or language models on accelerators. We study the
opposite end, sub-kilobyte MLPs on a single CPU thread, where overhead is visible in
isolation. Unlike capacity comparisons, we keep parameters constant and change only structure.
