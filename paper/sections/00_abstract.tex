\begin{abstract}
Architecture comparisons often use parameter counts or FLOPs as proxies for speed, but deployed systems are judged
by latency. We benchmark six small neural architectures, three single-paradigm models (sequential, parallel, modular)
and three hybrids combining depth, breadth, and modularity, all held to 385 parameters ($\pm$0.5\%) and evaluated on
three  regression tasks with five seeds each. A pilot study suggested a \pilotSpeedup$\times$ speedup for
modular networks. An examination traced it to a cross-framework confound and uninformative inputs, and we present it as
a case study in benchmarking pitfalls. In the corrected study, every topology reaches the noise floor on simple
tasks. On a composition task, a staged modular hybrid lowers test error by \chainHBGain\% relative to a
sequential baseline ($p<0.05$) and converges in far fewer epochs, at \latOneRatioHB$\times$ the single-sample
latency. Across models, single-sample latency is almost perfectly predicted by tensor operations per forward call
($R^2=\opsFitRsq$) rather than by parameter count, and at large batch sizes the ranking inverts in favor of
narrow-activation designs. Smaller architectures should therefore be compared at the deployment batch size,
with operator counts reported alongside parameters.
\end{abstract}
