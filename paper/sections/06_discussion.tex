\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Which Architecture Should a Practitioner Pick?}
The results support a simple decision rule for small models on latency-sensitive paths.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{If the target has no known structure, keep it shallow and minimize operators.} On the linear and
curved tasks every topology reached the noise floor, so the cheapest one, a single wide hidden layer with three
operations, becomes the best choice. It trains fastest per step and has the lowest batch-1 latency.
\ifcompact
\item \emph{If the target is compositional and its stages are known, a staged modular hybrid can be worth its
overhead:} \chainHBGain\% lower error at \latOneRatioHB$\times$ the batch-1 latency, attractive for batched inference only.
\else
\item \emph{If the target is compositional and its stages are known, a staged modular hybrid is worth its
overhead.} \HB cut chained-task excess error by \chainExcessRatio$\times$ at \stepRatioHB$\times$ the training cost per
step and \latOneRatioHB$\times$ the batch-1 latency, a modest accuracy gain (\chainHBGain\%) at a real cost. For offline or
batched inference, that trade can be worthwhile; for per-sample real-time loops it usually is not.
\fi
\item \emph{Measure latency at the deployment batch size.} The ranking at batch 1 (operator-bound) and at batch
4096 (memory-bound) differ. A benchmark at one batch size can recommend the incorrect model for another.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Target Scaling Can Masquerade as Architecture}
\ifcompact
On unscaled targets (an earlier version of this study), the hybrids converged far faster and \HB appeared
43\% better than Sequential on the chained task. Standardizing the targets removed most of the convergence gap
and shrank the advantage to \chainHBGain\%: like the pilot's framework confound, a protocol choice produced an
apparent architecture effect.
\else
An earlier version of this study trained on unscaled targets, whose variance reaches 229 on the curved task. There,
the single-paradigm models converged far more slowly than the hybrids (69 vs.\ 8 epochs on the curved task), and
\HB appeared 43\% better than Sequential on the chained task. Standardizing the targets removed nearly all of the
convergence gap and shrank the chained-task advantage to \chainHBGain\%. Much of the apparent hybrid benefit was
therefore an optimization effect: the additive, multi-path hybrids coped better with badly scaled targets. This is a
second example, after the pilot, of a protocol choice producing an apparent architecture effect.
\fi

\subsection{Lessons for Small-Scale Benchmarking}
The pilot study shows how easily a benchmark can produce a confident, wrong
conclusion. A few practices catch such errors and are cheap enough for any student or practitioner to adopt. First,
include a \emph{null control}: a pair that should show no difference, such as our identical Sequential and
Modular networks. Ours served several roles: it exposed the pilot's framework confound, flagged CPU contention during
training, and revealed module-call overhead that operation counts alone miss. Second, hold the framework and training loop fixed so that timing measures the model and not
the software stack~\cite{shi2016benchmarking}. Third, check accuracy against trivial baselines: a model whose loss equals the target variance
has learned nothing~\cite{lipton2019troubling}. Fourth, standardize targets before comparing training dynamics, so that
optimization difficulty is not mistaken for an architecture effect.
\fi

\subsection{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\ifcompact
\textbf{Scale.} The models are tiny and the tasks one-dimensional, by design: this isolates topology from capacity. Per-operator
overhead will matter less for larger models, where compute and memory traffic dominate~\cite{sze2017efficient}.
\else
\textbf{Scale.} The models are tiny ($\approx$385 parameters) and the tasks are one-dimensional. This is
deliberate, as it isolates the topology from overall capacity and makes the five-seed run cheap. It also means the
absolute latencies are dominated by per-operator overhead in a way that will shrink, though not vanish, for larger
models, where compute and memory traffic take over~\cite{sze2017efficient}. The ranking of architectures at
batch~4096, where arithmetic begins to matter, is a better guide to larger-scale behavior than batch~1.
\fi

\ifcompact
\textbf{Hardware and runtime.} All timings come from one Apple M5 CPU (eager PyTorch and \texttt{torch.compile}); other
runtimes and hardware differ, so we report trends, not absolute numbers.
\else
\textbf{Hardware and runtime.} All timings come from one Apple M5 CPU. We measured eager PyTorch and
\texttt{torch.compile}; other runtimes (ONNX Runtime, TensorRT) and other hardware (GPUs, microcontrollers, neural
accelerators) have different overhead profiles. We therefore present latency \emph{trends} across architectures, not
absolute numbers to be quoted for other platforms.
\fi

\textbf{Task design.} The chained task was written by the same author who designed \HB, so \HB's structural
match to it is an intentional best case for modular inductive bias, not an unbiased test. We do not claim
that staged modularity helps on arbitrary compositional problems, only that it \emph{can} when the
decomposition is known.

\textbf{Statistics.} Five seeds detect large effects but not small ones. We report Welch's $t$-tests
without multiple-comparison correction, so isolated marginal results should be read as suggestive.
Hyperparameters (learning rate, epochs, batch size) were fixed for all models rather than tuned per
architecture, which may disadvantage architectures that prefer other settings.
